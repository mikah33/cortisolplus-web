## What works best to raise HRV?

**Sleep first, then aerobic exercise, then stress management.** These basics beat expensive supplements and gadgets.

Heart rate variability (HRV) is the small change in time between your heartbeats. Higher HRV usually means your nervous system is more flexible and better at bouncing back.

If you want to raise your HRV, you want things that actually work, not guesswork. This post ranks the evidence-based options by how big their effect is, so you know where to focus.

## What does HRV actually measure?

HRV shows the balance between two parts of your nervous system:

- the **sympathetic** side ("fight or flight")
- the **parasympathetic** side ("rest and digest")

When you're stressed, sick or overtrained, HRV usually drops. When you're well-rested and recovered, it usually rises.

### Everyone's normal is different

Some healthy people have an HRV of 20ms. Others have 100ms.

What matters most is **your own trend over weeks and months**, not how you compare with others.

## 1. The biggest lever: sleep

**Sleep has the largest and most consistent effect on HRV.**

Studies show that not getting enough sleep can drop HRV by 10-30%. Even one night of poor sleep lowers HRV the next day.

What works:

- **A steady sleep schedule.** Going to bed and waking at the same time every day improves HRV more than an irregular schedule, even with the same total hours.
- **7-9 hours a night.** Most adults need this range for HRV recovery.
- **The right bedroom.** Cool (65-68°F), dark and quiet rooms support the deeper sleep stages that boost HRV.

If you're trying to [improve your HRV](/hrv/how-to-improve/), put sleep before anything else. Its effect is bigger than most supplements or expensive gadgets.

## 2. Aerobic exercise: proven, but the amount matters

**Regular aerobic training raises resting HRV by 5-20% over 8-12 weeks in most studies.** But timing and intensity matter.

Aerobic exercise is steady movement that gets you breathing harder, like brisk walking, cycling or jogging.

**What raises HRV:**

- Moderate aerobic exercise (60-70% of your max heart rate), done regularly
- Easy recovery days between hard sessions
- At least 3-4 sessions a week

**What lowers HRV:**

- Hard exercise without enough recovery
- Overtraining and too few rest days
- Very hard workouts right before you measure HRV

Your HRV usually drops right after hard exercise and bounces back as you recover. If it stays low for several days, you might need more rest.

Athletes who track HRV often adjust how hard they train based on their morning readings.

## 3. Stress management and breathing

**Long-term stress steadily lowers HRV. The good news: some relaxation methods can raise it in a way you can measure.**

- **Slow breathing** (around 6 breaths per minute) raises HRV within minutes. Studies show that regular practice of 10-20 minutes a day can raise your baseline HRV by 10-15% over 4-8 weeks.
- **Meditation and mindfulness** also show steady but smaller effects, usually 5-10% increases after 8 weeks of regular practice.
- **Biofeedback training**, which teaches you to control your breathing and heart rate, can give similar improvements.

These methods work partly by switching on your parasympathetic ("rest and digest") nervous system. That system is linked to both HRV and how your body handles cortisol (your main stress hormone).

If you also want to [lower cortisol](/cortisol/lower/), stress management helps with both.

<ZenTip image="/app-ui/zen_new_breathe.webp">
  Try breathing in for about <strong>5 seconds</strong> and out for about <strong>5 seconds</strong>. That's around 6 breaths a minute.
</ZenTip>

## 4. Lifestyle changes with moderate evidence

These show promise, but the effects are smaller or less consistent:

- **Less alcohol:** even moderate drinking (2+ drinks) lowers HRV that night and into the next day. Not drinking, or keeping to 1 drink, improves recovery numbers.
- **Weight loss:** for people with obesity, losing 5-10% of body weight usually raises HRV by 5-15%.
- **Omega-3 (fish oil):** 1-2g a day of EPA+DHA shows small HRV increases (3-8%) in some studies, especially in people who don't get much from food.
- **Sauna:** regular sauna use (2-3x a week) may raise HRV by 5-10%, possibly through better heart and blood vessel health and adapting to stress.
- **Cold exposure:** cold showers or ice baths show mixed results. Some studies find short-term HRV increases, others find none. If there is an effect, it seems small.

Thinking about a supplement like fish oil? Ask a doctor or pharmacist first.

## What probably doesn't work?

Despite the marketing, these have weak or no evidence for raising HRV:

- Most supplements (magnesium, vitamin D, adaptogens) don't have good HRV studies
- Special diets (keto, paleo, intermittent fasting) show no steady HRV changes, apart from any weight loss
- Grounding or "earthing" mats
- Most recovery devices, beyond simple sleep and stress tracking

**The basics (sleep, exercise and stress management) do more than expensive supplements or gadgets.**
