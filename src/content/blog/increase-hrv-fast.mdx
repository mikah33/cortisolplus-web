## What works best to raise HRV?

**Sleep first, then aerobic exercise, then stress management.** These basics beat expensive supplements and gadgets.

Heart rate variability (HRV) is the small change in time between your heartbeats. Higher HRV usually means your nervous system is more flexible and better at bouncing back.

If you want to raise your HRV, you want things that actually work, not guesswork. This post goes through the main options, starting with the basics, so you know where to focus.

## What does HRV actually measure?

HRV shows the balance between two parts of your nervous system:

- the **sympathetic** side ("fight or flight")
- the **parasympathetic** side ("rest and digest")

When you're stressed, sick or overtrained, HRV usually drops. When you're well-rested and recovered, it usually rises.

### Everyone's normal is different

Healthy people can have very different HRV numbers. Some run much lower or higher than others.

What matters most is **your own trend over weeks and months**, not how you compare with others.

## 1. Start with sleep

**Sleep is one of the most important basics for HRV.**

Not getting enough sleep can lower HRV. Even one night of poor sleep can lower it the next day.

What works:

- **A steady sleep schedule.** Going to bed and waking at about the same time every day may help.
- **7-9 hours a night.** Most adults need this much sleep.
- **The right bedroom.** A cool, dark and quiet room can make it easier to sleep well.

If you're trying to [improve your HRV](/hrv/how-to-improve/), start with sleep. It's a better bet than most supplements or expensive gadgets.

<ZenTip image="/app-ui/zen_new_tour_sleep.webp">
  Pick one wake-up time and keep it <strong>on weekends too</strong>. A steady schedule is the easiest sleep win.
</ZenTip>

## 2. Aerobic exercise: the amount matters

**Regular aerobic training can raise resting HRV over time.** But how hard you train and how much you rest matter.

Aerobic exercise is steady movement that gets you breathing harder, like brisk walking, cycling or jogging.

**What raises HRV:**

- Moderate aerobic exercise, done regularly
- Easy recovery days between hard sessions

**What lowers HRV:**

- Hard exercise without enough recovery
- Overtraining and too few rest days
- Very hard workouts right before you measure HRV

Your HRV usually drops right after hard exercise and bounces back as you recover. If it stays low for several days, you might need more rest.

Athletes who track HRV often adjust how hard they train based on their morning readings.

## 3. Stress management and breathing

**Long-term stress can lower HRV. The good news: some relaxation methods may help raise it.**

- **Slow breathing** can raise HRV while you're doing it. Whether regular practice raises your everyday baseline is less clear.
- **Meditation and mindfulness** may help some people, but studies are mixed.
- **Biofeedback training**, which teaches you to control your breathing and heart rate, may help in a similar way.

These methods are thought to work partly by switching on your parasympathetic ("rest and digest") nervous system.

If you also want to [lower cortisol](/cortisol/lower/), stress management is part of that guide too.

<ZenTip image="/app-ui/zen_new_breathe.webp">
  Try breathing in for about <strong>5 seconds</strong> and out for about <strong>5 seconds</strong>. That's around 6 breaths a minute.
</ZenTip>

## 4. Lifestyle changes with weaker evidence

These may help some people, but the evidence is weaker or less consistent:

- **Less alcohol:** drinking can lower HRV that night and into the next day. Drinking less may help your recovery numbers.
- **Weight loss:** for people with obesity, losing weight may raise HRV.
- **Omega-3 (fish oil):** some studies find small HRV increases, but results vary.
- **Sauna:** regular sauna use may raise HRV for some people, but the evidence is limited.
- **Cold exposure:** cold showers or ice baths show mixed results. Some studies find short-term HRV increases, others find none. If there is an effect, it seems small.

Thinking about a supplement like fish oil? Ask a doctor or pharmacist first.

## What probably doesn't work?

Despite the marketing, these have weak or no evidence for raising HRV:

- Most supplements (magnesium, vitamin D, adaptogens) don't have good HRV studies
- Special diets (keto, paleo, intermittent fasting) show no steady HRV changes, apart from any weight loss
- Grounding or "earthing" mats
- Most recovery devices, beyond simple sleep and stress tracking

**The basics (sleep, exercise and stress management) do more than expensive supplements or gadgets.**
