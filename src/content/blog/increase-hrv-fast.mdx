Heart rate variability (HRV) measures the variation in time between heartbeats. Higher HRV generally means your nervous system is more flexible and resilient. If you're wondering how to increase HRV, you want interventions that actually work—not just guesswork.

This post ranks the evidence-based tactics by their effect size, so you know where to focus your energy.

## What HRV actually measures

HRV reflects the balance between your sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest") nervous systems. When you're stressed, sick, or overtrained, HRV typically drops. When you're well-rested and recovered, it usually rises.

Individual baseline HRV varies widely—some healthy people have an HRV of 20ms, others 100ms. What matters most is your personal trend over weeks and months, not comparing yourself to others.

## The biggest lever: sleep quality and quantity

Sleep has the largest, most consistent effect on HRV. Studies show that sleep deprivation can drop HRV by 10-30%. Even one night of poor sleep reduces HRV the next day.

What works:

- **Consistent sleep schedule**: Going to bed and waking at the same time daily improves HRV more than irregular sleep patterns, even with the same total hours
- **7-9 hours nightly**: Most adults need this range for HRV recovery
- **Sleep environment**: Cool (65-68°F), dark, and quiet rooms support deeper sleep stages that boost HRV

If you're trying to [improve your HRV](/hrv/how-to-improve/), prioritize sleep before anything else. The effect size is bigger than most supplements or expensive gadgets.

## Aerobic exercise: proven but dose-dependent

Regular aerobic training increases resting HRV by 5-20% over 8-12 weeks in most studies. But timing and intensity matter.

**What raises HRV:**
- Moderate aerobic exercise (60-70% max heart rate) done consistently
- Easy recovery days between hard sessions
- At least 3-4 sessions per week

**What lowers HRV:**
- Intense exercise without adequate recovery
- Overtraining and insufficient rest days
- Very hard workouts right before measuring HRV

Your HRV typically drops immediately after hard exercise and rebounds during recovery. If it stays low for several days, you might need more rest. Athletes who monitor HRV often adjust training intensity based on their morning readings.

## Stress management and breathing techniques

Chronic psychological stress consistently lowers HRV. The good news: specific relaxation techniques can raise it measurably.

**Slow breathing** (around 6 breaths per minute) increases HRV acutely within minutes. Studies show regular practice—10-20 minutes daily—can raise baseline HRV by 10-15% over 4-8 weeks.

**Meditation and mindfulness** also show consistent but smaller effects, typically 5-10% increases after 8 weeks of regular practice.

**Biofeedback training** that teaches you to control your breathing and heart rate can produce similar improvements.

These techniques work partly by activating your parasympathetic nervous system, which is linked to both HRV and cortisol regulation. If you're looking to [lower cortisol](/cortisol/lower/) alongside improving HRV, stress management hits both targets.

## Lifestyle factors with moderate evidence

Several other interventions show promise, though effect sizes are smaller or less consistent:

**Alcohol reduction**: Even moderate drinking (2+ drinks) lowers HRV that night and into the next day. Abstaining or limiting to 1 drink improves recovery metrics.

**Weight loss**: For people with obesity, losing 5-10% body weight typically raises HRV by 5-15%.

**Omega-3 supplementation**: Fish oil at 1-2g daily EPA+DHA shows modest HRV increases (3-8%) in some studies, especially in people with low baseline intake.

**Sauna use**: Regular sauna bathing (2-3x weekly) may increase HRV by 5-10%, possibly through improved cardiovascular function and stress adaptation.

**Cold exposure**: Cold showers or ice baths show mixed results. Some studies find short-term HRV increases, others find none. Effect size appears small if present.

## What probably doesn't work

Despite marketing claims, these have weak or no evidence for raising HRV:

- Most supplements (magnesium, vitamin D, adaptogens) lack quality HRV studies
- Specific diets (keto, paleo, intermittent fasting) show no consistent HRV changes independent of weight loss
- Grounding or earthing mats
- Most recovery devices beyond simple sleep and stress tracking

The fundamentals—sleep, exercise, and stress management—deliver bigger results than expensive supplements or gadgets.
