## Why don't my apps agree?

Each app looks at different things.

One app only checks your heart rate variability (HRV) while you sit still. Another mixes in sleep, resting heart rate and how active you were.

They also measure at different times, and they turn the result into a number in their own way. So two apps can disagree about the same night, and **neither one is broken**.

None of them measure stress hormones. They're all estimates from heart, sleep and activity data.

## What makes the numbers different?

### They read different signals

Garmin's number comes from HRV. Apple mixes training, sleep and overnight vitals. Cortisol+ counts HRV and sleep the most, then resting heart rate, then smaller things like breathing rate ([see the full list](/how-it-works/)).

So a hard workout or a short night can move one score a lot and barely move another.

### They measure at different times

Garmin measures when you're still. A busy, active afternoon might not show up at all.

Apple's Readiness keeps updating through the day. One app's overnight number and another app's all-day number can be different for good reason.

<ZenPicture src="/app-ui/start/zen_watch.webp" caption="Same watch, same night, different apps: different numbers." />

### "HRV" isn't always the same thing

Apple Health has long saved HRV as a measure called **SDNN**. Many other wearables use one called **RMSSD**. They're worked out differently, so you can't compare the values directly. More on this in [HRV on Apple Watch](/hrv/apple-watch/).

### Each app learns "your normal" its own way

Most scores compare you with your own past. Cortisol+ starts with general defaults, then mixes in your own data after 10 readings. It's fully based on you after 28.

That's why a new app, or a new watch, can look a bit off for the first few weeks.

## How do I compare apps fairly?

**Watch the trend inside each app.** Going from 40 to 60 over two weeks in one app means something. A 40 in one app next to a 60 in another means almost nothing.

1. Wear the same watch the way you normally do, including to bed.
2. Write down each app's scale, and which way is "good".
3. Compare overnight with overnight, not one app's morning with another's evening.
4. When they disagree, open Apple Health and look at the HRV, resting heart rate and sleep for those days.
5. Jot down what was going on: late meals, alcohol, illness, travel. Our free [stress and sleep journal](/resources/stress-sleep-journal/) helps.
6. After a week or two, keep the app that explains things best, not the one with the "nicest" number.

## What a score can't tell you

A score shows how your body's signals compare with your normal. It can't tell you **why** they changed. It can't diagnose anything, and it can't measure cortisol, which needs a lab test ([cortisol testing options](/cortisol/test/)).

Use it as a nudge to look at your sleep, training and routine. If something worries you, talk to a doctor.
