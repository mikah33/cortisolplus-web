Stress scores vary widely between apps because developers make fundamentally different choices about what to measure, when to measure it, and how to translate raw data into a single number. Understanding these differences helps you interpret your results and choose tools that match your goals.

## Different inputs produce different scores

Apps measure stress using different physiological signals. Some rely solely on heart rate variability from wrist-worn sensors, while others incorporate resting heart rate, movement patterns, or sleep data. Because each input captures a different aspect of your physiology, two apps analyzing the same time period can produce completely different scores. An app focused on HRV during rest might show low stress while an app tracking elevated heart rate during activity shows high stress—both accurately reflecting their chosen measurement but answering different questions.

Apple Watch, for example, collects heart rate and motion data throughout the day. According to Apple Support documentation, the device uses heart rate variability and other sensor inputs to generate insights, though the exact calculation method is proprietary. When you compare results across platforms, remember that no universal standard defines what a stress score must include.

## Measurement timing changes everything

When an app collects data dramatically affects the score it generates. Some apps sample continuously throughout the day, averaging all measurements into a single daily score. Others focus on specific windows—first thing in the morning, during designated breathing exercises, or only during detected rest periods. A morning-only measurement might miss afternoon stress spikes entirely, while all-day averaging might dilute acute stress events into a moderate score.

Cortisol Plus, for instance, documents its approach on its How It Works page: it uses heart rate variability data from Apple Watch, focusing on measurements during rest periods to assess recovery capacity. This method intentionally excludes active periods, producing scores that reflect different physiology than an app sampling continuously. Neither approach is wrong, but they answer different questions about your stress patterns.

## Scoring definitions lack standardization

Even when two apps measure the same input at the same time, they can assign vastly different numerical scores because scoring scales are arbitrary. One app might use 0-100 with higher numbers indicating more stress, another might use 1-10 with lower numbers indicating worse stress, and a third might flip the scale entirely to show "resilience" instead. Some apps derive scores from millisecond-level HRV values, others from frequency-domain analysis, and still others from proprietary algorithms combining multiple factors.

No regulatory body has established universal cutoffs for what constitutes "low," "moderate," or "high" stress scores. Each developer defines these thresholds independently. A score of 65 might be considered moderate in one app and high in another, not because your physiology changed but because the scales use different reference ranges.

## Practical interpretation steps

To make sense of stress scores across different apps, track trends within a single app rather than comparing absolute numbers between apps. A score moving from 40 to 60 over two weeks within one app provides useful information even if you don't know how that score compares to another platform. Review each app's documentation to understand what inputs it uses and when it samples data—many apps explain their methodology in support pages or settings. ## Limitations to remember

Stress scores from consumer wellness apps are not medical measurements. These tools do not diagnose stress disorders, measure cortisol hormone levels, or meet clinical accuracy standards. They provide general wellness insights based on patterns in heart rate and movement data. If you have health concerns, consult a healthcare provider rather than relying on app scores for medical decisions.
