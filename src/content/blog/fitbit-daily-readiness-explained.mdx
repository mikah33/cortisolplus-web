## What is the Fitbit Daily Readiness Score?

**The Fitbit Daily Readiness Score is a number from 1 to 100 that shows up each morning on your [Fitbit](/fitbit/) device.** It aims to tell you if your body is ready for a hard workout or needs time to recover.

It's more than a step count. It pulls together three kinds of data:

- heart rate variability (HRV)
- sleep quality
- recent activity

So if you've ever wondered whether to skip leg day or push through, this score tries to answer with data instead of guesswork.

## What goes into the score?

Every morning, Fitbit looks at three main things:

- **Heart rate variability (HRV):** the change in time between heartbeats, measured while you sleep.
- **Sleep:** total sleep time, sleep stages and overall sleep quality.
- **Recent activity:** how much you've exercised in the past few days, compared with your normal.

Each one is weighted, and Fitbit turns them into one score.

- **A high score (above 65 or so)** suggests you've recovered and can handle a hard session.
- **A low score (below 30)** means your body is still dealing with tiredness, stress or poor sleep.

The score adjusts over time as the app learns your patterns. "High HRV" for you may be different from someone else's. So Fitbit compares your readings with **your own 30-day baseline** (your usual level).

## How does HRV change the score?

**[Heart rate variability](/fitbit/hrv/) is the biggest single part of the score.**

- When your nervous system is calm and recovered, HRV tends to be higher.
- When you're stressed, sick or overtrained, HRV drops.

Fitbit tracks HRV on its own while you sleep, using the light-based heart rate sensor on your wrist. It uses a measure called RMSSD, which looks at short-term changes between beats. Then it compares last night's HRV with your recent average.

A sharp drop in HRV usually lowers your score. A higher-than-usual HRV raises it.

That's why your score can be low after a night of drinking, a stressful day, or while you're fighting off a cold. It can happen even if you slept a full eight hours.

## Why do sleep length and sleep quality both matter?

Sleep counts in two ways: **how much** you slept and **how well** you slept.

Fitbit tracks your time in light, deep and REM sleep.

- More deep and REM sleep than usual raises your score.
- Tossing and turning all night, or only five hours of sleep, lowers it.

The app also looks at how regular your sleep is. Going to bed and waking up at about the same time each day helps keep your score steady.

An irregular sleep schedule confuses your body clock (circadian rhythm). It can pull readiness down even if your total sleep looks okay.

One bad night won't wreck your score if your HRV and activity are fine. But several short or broken nights in a row will push it lower over time.

## How does recent exercise count?

Your workouts over the past few days are the third part of the score. Fitbit compares how active you've been lately with your normal.

**More hard exercise than usual lowers the score.** If you ran a half-marathon yesterday, or did three hard lifting sessions this week, your activity load is high. That tells Fitbit your body may need more recovery time.

**A lazy few days doesn't mean "go all out".** If you've barely moved for several days, the score might stay high. That doesn't mean you should jump straight into a max-effort workout. The score shows how recovered you are, not how fit you are.

The activity part also counts **Active Zone Minutes**: minutes spent with your heart rate raised. More Active Zone Minutes means more strain on your body, which affects whether you're ready for another hard session.

These are guides, not rules. Some athletes ignore low scores and train anyway because of their schedule. Others use the score as permission to take a guilt-free rest day.

**The score works best when you track it over weeks or months.** Does it drop after late nights? Does it stay high even when you feel wiped out? Patterns like these show how well the score matches how recovered you really are.
