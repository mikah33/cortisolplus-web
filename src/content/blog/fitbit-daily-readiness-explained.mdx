## What is the Fitbit Daily Readiness Score?

**The Fitbit Daily Readiness Score is a number from 1 to 100 that you see in the Google Health app (formerly the Fitbit app), using data from your [Fitbit](/fitbit/) device.** It aims to tell you if your body is ready for a hard workout or needs time to recover.

It's more than a step count. It pulls together three kinds of data:

- heart rate variability (HRV)
- resting heart rate
- recent sleep

So if you've ever wondered whether to skip leg day or push through, this score tries to answer with data instead of guesswork.

## What goes into the score?

Fitbit combines three main things:

- **Heart rate variability (HRV):** the change in time between heartbeats, measured while you sleep.
- **Resting heart rate:** your heart rate when you're fully at rest.
- **Sleep:** how much and how well you've slept recently.

Fitbit turns them into one score.

- **A high score (65 or above)** suggests you've recovered and can handle a hard session.
- **A low score (29 or below)** means your body may still be dealing with tiredness, stress or poor sleep.

"High HRV" for you may be different from someone else's. So it makes sense to compare your score with **your own usual level**, not with other people's.

## How does HRV change the score?

**[Heart rate variability](/fitbit/hrv/) is one of the score's three inputs.**

- When your nervous system is calm and recovered, HRV tends to be higher.
- When you're stressed, sick or overtrained, HRV drops.

Fitbit tracks HRV on its own while you sleep, using the light-based heart rate sensor on your wrist. It uses a measure called RMSSD, which looks at short-term changes between beats. A sharp drop in HRV compared with your usual level can pull your score down.

That's why your score can be low after a night of drinking, a stressful day, or while you're fighting off a cold. It can happen even if you slept a full eight hours.

## Why do sleep length and sleep quality both matter?

Sleep counts in two ways: **how much** you slept and **how well** you slept.

Fitbit tracks your time in light, deep and REM sleep.

- Short or broken sleep tends to pull readiness down.
- Several short or broken nights in a row can push it lower over time.

A regular bedtime is still a good habit. It helps you get enough sleep night after night.

## How does recent exercise count?

**Exercise isn't listed as a separate input.** The score comes from HRV, resting heart rate and sleep.

**Hard training can still show up.** After a half-marathon or several hard sessions, your HRV may dip and your resting heart rate may rise. That can lower your score and hint that you need more recovery time.

**A lazy few days doesn't mean "go all out".** If you've barely moved for several days, the score might stay high. That doesn't mean you should jump straight into a max-effort workout. The score shows how recovered you are, not how fit you are.

These are guides, not rules. Some athletes ignore low scores and train anyway because of their schedule. Others use the score as permission to take a guilt-free rest day.

**The score works best when you track it over weeks or months.** Does it drop after late nights? Does it stay high even when you feel wiped out? Patterns like these show how well the score matches how recovered you really are.
