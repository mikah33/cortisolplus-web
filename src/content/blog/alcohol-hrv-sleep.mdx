**Alcohol intake has been associated with lower overnight HRV and higher resting heart rate in wearable studies.** If you notice this pattern after drinking, it can be useful context. Your Watch cannot determine the cause of an individual change or measure your cortisol level.

Cortisol+ publishes this guide. We did not run these studies, and their findings do not validate our app's score.

## What the research found

A [2026 study in PLOS Digital Health](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pdig.0001284) analyzed 5,109,185 person-days from 20,968 participants. Higher alcohol intake was associated with higher nocturnal resting heart rate, lower HRV and shorter sleep. The researchers examined within-person patterns and adjusted for several factors, including drinking frequency and season.

This was observational research using wearable data, not an experiment proving the cause of every change. It used WHOOP data, received WHOOP support and included company employees among its authors. Its findings should not be converted into an exact prediction for an Apple Watch user or a Cortisol+ score.

An [earlier observational study of 4,098 Finnish employees](https://mental.jmir.org/2018/1/e23) also examined alcohol intake and autonomic measures during the first hours of sleep. Together, these studies support paying attention to alcohol as part of the context around a reading—not diagnosing yourself from a graph.

## How much will my HRV drop after drinking?

There is no fixed number we can promise. People, doses, timing and measurement methods differ. A research average is not a personal forecast, and an app score is not the same unit as HRV measured in milliseconds.

For example, do not interpret a ten-point change in a wellness score as a ten-millisecond change in HRV. Check the metric and units on the screen you are reading.

## How to review your Apple Watch data

Use observations from your normal routine; there is no need to drink to create a comparison.

1. Find the original HRV records in Apple Health and check their dates, timestamps and source.
2. Review sleep and resting heart rate for the same period, checking whether each summary covers the same hours.
3. Note alcohol timing if relevant, plus unusual exercise, travel, illness or disrupted sleep.
4. Compare several similar nights using the same device and measurement method.
5. Keep the conclusion modest: “These changed together” is different from “This proves what caused the change.”

Apple documents its SDNN method in its [heart measurement technical paper](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Calorimetry_Activity_on_Apple_Watch_November_2024.pdf). Do not compare that number directly with a different wearable's RMSSD as if they were identical measurements.

For a more detailed checklist, read [low HRV during sleep on Apple Watch](/blog/hrv-during-sleep/). If the record is missing, start with [HRV troubleshooting](/blog/apple-watch-hrv-not-updating/).

## Can a normal HRV reading tell me drinking was harmless?

No. A wearable summarizes selected signals. A reading near your usual range does not establish that alcohol had no effects or that drinking is safe for you. The [National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism](https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/alcohols-effects-health/alcohols-effects-body) describes effects that extend beyond the measurements shown by a Watch.

## Does this show a cortisol spike?

No. A lower HRV value cannot confirm elevated cortisol. [Cortisol tests](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/cortisol-test/) measure the hormone in blood, saliva or urine. Read our [Apple Watch cortisol guide](/stress/apple-watch/) for the difference between sensor readings, wellness interpretations and hormone tests.
