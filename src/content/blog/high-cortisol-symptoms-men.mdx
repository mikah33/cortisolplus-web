Fatigue, lower sex drive or a change in body composition does not establish high cortisol in a man. These experiences can have several explanations, and a wearable stress score does not distinguish them.

## Symptoms are a starting point

The [NHS discussion of male midlife symptoms](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/male-menopause/) explains that sexual, mood and energy changes may relate to psychological factors, lifestyle or medical conditions. Testosterone deficiency is one possible clinical question, not a conclusion that follows from tiredness or stress.

The claim that cortisol and testosterone compete in a simple one-up, one-down relationship cannot diagnose what is happening in an individual. Do not choose supplements or hormone treatment from that explanation.

## When cortisol is part of a medical assessment

Cushing's syndrome can include muscle weakness, easy bruising and changes in body-fat distribution; men may also have sexual or fertility changes. [NIDDK describes the condition](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/cushings-syndrome). A symptom list is not sufficient to diagnose it.

## Prepare useful information

Describe when changes began, whether they affect daily life or training, and any medicines or supplements. Bring actual test results if you already have them. Ask what the clinician is investigating before ordering or interpreting a hormone test.

## What wearable trends add

You can review sleep or HRV records as context, but an app cannot show that cortisol suppressed testosterone or confirm recovery from a hormone problem. Cortisol+ has no validated threshold for diagnosing overtraining or a testosterone disorder. See [how the wellness score works](/how-it-works/) before using it in a training decision.
