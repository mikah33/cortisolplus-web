You're exhausted but can't fall asleep. Or you fall asleep fine, then wake at 3 a.m. with your mind racing. Your body is tired, but your brain won't switch off.

This pattern is often called cortisol insomnia. **It happens when your stress hormone stays high when it should be dropping.**

Cortisol insomnia isn't a formal medical diagnosis. But it describes a real problem: high cortisol getting in the way of normal sleep. Knowing how cortisol and sleep work together helps you figure out what might help.

## How does cortisol normally work with sleep?

**Cortisol follows a daily rhythm (the circadian pattern).** In healthy sleep, cortisol is:

- Lowest around the middle of the night
- Rising in the early morning hours toward waking
- Highest 30–60 minutes after you wake up
- Slowly dropping through the day

This pattern supports your sleep-wake cycle. Low night-time cortisol lets your body relax and go into deep sleep ([StatPearls: Physiology, Cortisol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)). The morning rise helps you wake up and feel alert.

When stress goes on for a long time, this rhythm gets thrown off. Cortisol may stay high into the evening and night. Even if you're physically worn out, high cortisol keeps your body alert.

Your nervous system acts as if there's still a threat to deal with. Long-term insomnia is linked with the HPA axis (your stress-hormone system) being active around the clock. Researchers call this physiological hyperarousal, meaning the body stays "switched on" ([Vgontzas et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11502812/)).

## Why does high cortisol block sleep?

**Cortisol is part of your "fight or flight" response.** When it's high, your body puts staying alert ahead of resting. Here's what happens:

- **Your heart rate stays up.** Your heart and blood vessels stay switched on, so it's hard to relax.
- **Blood sugar stays ready.** Your body keeps glucose (sugar) on hand for quick energy.
- **Your brain stays on alert.** Your mind keeps going, and worry loops continue.

**This is the "wired but tired" feeling.** Your body has been running on stress hormones all day, so you feel drained. But those same hormones block the shutdown you need for sleep.

Long-term stress can also cut down deep sleep and REM sleep, the stages that make sleep refreshing. You might sleep seven or eight hours and still wake up feeling like you barely rested.

## What keeps cortisol high at night?

**Several things can keep cortisol up when it should be dropping:**

- **Ongoing work or life stress.** Deadlines, conflict or worry that don't let up.
- **Poor sleep itself.** Lack of sleep can raise cortisol, which makes a cycle. Even one night of short sleep can raise the next evening's cortisol ([Leproult et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865)).
- **Late caffeine or hard evening workouts.** Both can raise cortisol in the evening.
- **An irregular sleep schedule.** This confuses your body clock and your cortisol timing.
- **Blood sugar dips.** Low blood sugar at night can trigger cortisol release.
- **Bright light at night.** Light from screens late in the evening can delay sleep.

Some medical conditions, like Cushing's syndrome, cause truly high cortisol around the clock. **But cortisol insomnia usually comes from lifestyle and long-term stress patterns, not disease.**

## What helps lower night-time cortisol?

**The goal is to support your natural rhythm so cortisol drops in the evening.** Approaches with evidence behind them:

1. **Keep steady sleep and wake times.** Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day helps keep your body clock steady. Even on weekends, try to stay within an hour of your normal schedule.
2. **Get morning light.** Bright light early in the day strengthens your body clock. That helps cortisol peak in the morning and drop at night.
3. **Build an evening wind-down routine.** Give yourself 60–90 minutes to shift from activity to sleep. Dim the lights, cut screen time and do calm things. This helps your body get ready for sleep.
4. **Keep blood sugar steady.** Eat balanced meals with protein. Avoid long stretches without food that might cause blood sugar to drop at night. A small snack with protein before bed helps some people.
5. **Practice stress relief.** Deep breathing, meditation or progressive muscle relaxation (tensing and relaxing muscles one group at a time) can help you wind down. Try 10–15 minutes before bed.
6. **Limit late stimulants.** Stop caffeine by early afternoon. Move hard workouts to the morning or midday when you can.

## How can I track the pattern?

**Tracking can show your own pattern.** Sleep problems can feel constant, but they're often worse on certain days or follow triggers you can predict.

Noticing what affects your sleep helps you tell whether changes are working.

- Our [sleep habits quiz](/tools/sleep-debt-cortisol/) helps you reflect on your hours, wake-ups and evenings. It doesn't estimate your cortisol.
- Tools that track sleep quality, heart rate variability (HRV) and other body signals can show whether your night-time body signals are actually improving. [Sleep tracking features](/features/sleep-cortisol-tracking/) give you data beyond how you feel.

## Where should I start?

**Start with one or two changes, not everything at once.** Steady sleep-wake times and a wind-down routine are usually the best places to begin.
