You're exhausted but can't fall asleep. Or you fall asleep fine, then wake at 3 a.m. with your mind racing. Your body feels tired, but your brain won't shut off. This pattern is often called cortisol insomnia, and it happens when your stress hormone levels stay high when they should be dropping.

Cortisol insomnia isn't a formal medical diagnosis, but it describes a real problem: elevated cortisol interfering with normal sleep. Understanding how cortisol and sleep interact helps you figure out what might actually help.

## How cortisol normally works with sleep

Cortisol follows a daily rhythm called the circadian pattern. In healthy sleep, cortisol should be:

- Lowest around midnight to 2 a.m.
- Rising sharply in the early morning hours
- Peaking 30-60 minutes after you wake up
- Gradually declining through the day

This pattern supports your sleep-wake cycle. Low nighttime cortisol allows your body to relax and enter deep sleep ([StatPearls: Physiology, Cortisol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)). The morning spike helps you wake up and feel alert.

When stress becomes chronic, this rhythm gets disrupted. Cortisol may stay elevated into the evening and night. Even if you're physically exhausted, high cortisol keeps your body in a state of alertness. Your nervous system thinks there's still a threat to deal with—chronic insomnia is associated with round-the-clock activation of the HPA axis, a state researchers describe as physiological hyperarousal ([Vgontzas et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11502812/)).

## Why high cortisol blocks sleep

Cortisol is part of your "fight or flight" stress response. When it's elevated, your body prioritizes staying alert over resting. Here's what happens physically:

- **Heart rate stays elevated**: Your cardiovascular system remains activated, making it hard to relax
- **Body temperature stays higher**: Core temperature needs to drop for sleep initiation
- **Blood sugar remains mobilized**: Your body keeps glucose available for quick energy
- **Brain stays in alert mode**: Mental activity and worry loops continue

This is the "wired but tired" feeling. Your body has been running on stress hormones all day, so you feel depleted. But those same hormones prevent the shutdown you need for sleep.

Chronic stress also reduces deep sleep and REM sleep, the stages that make sleep restorative. You might sleep for seven or eight hours but wake up feeling like you barely rested.

## What causes nighttime cortisol elevation

Several factors can keep cortisol high when it should be dropping:

- **Chronic work or life stress**: Ongoing deadlines, conflict, or worry
- **Poor sleep itself**: Sleep deprivation raises next-day cortisol, creating a cycle—even a single night of short sleep can raise the following evening's cortisol ([Leproult et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865))
- **Late-day caffeine or intense exercise**: Both can spike cortisol in the evening
- **Irregular sleep schedule**: Confuses your circadian rhythm and cortisol timing
- **Blood sugar crashes**: Low blood sugar at night triggers cortisol release
- **Blue light exposure**: Screens before bed can disrupt the cortisol-melatonin balance

Some medical conditions like Cushing's syndrome cause truly elevated cortisol around the clock, but cortisol insomnia usually stems from lifestyle and chronic stress patterns rather than disease.

## What actually helps lower nighttime cortisol

The goal is to support your natural cortisol rhythm so it drops in the evening. Evidence-based approaches include:

**Consistent sleep-wake times**: Going to bed and waking up at the same time daily helps reset cortisol patterns. Even on weekends, try to stay within an hour of your normal schedule.

**Morning light exposure**: Bright light early in the day strengthens your circadian rhythm, which helps cortisol peak in the morning and drop at night.

**Evening wind-down routine**: Give yourself 60-90 minutes to transition from activity to sleep. Dim lights, reduce screen time, and do calming activities. This signals your body to begin the cortisol decline.

**Manage blood sugar**: Eat balanced meals with protein and avoid long fasting periods that might cause nighttime blood sugar drops. A small protein-containing snack before bed can help some people.

**Stress reduction practices**: Techniques like deep breathing, meditation, or progressive muscle relaxation have been shown to lower cortisol. Even 10-15 minutes before bed can help.

**Limit late-day stimulants**: Cut off caffeine by early afternoon. Move intense workouts to morning or midday when possible.

Because cortisol insomnia involves disrupted hormone timing, tracking can reveal your specific pattern. Sleep problems feel constant, but often they're worse on certain days or follow predictable triggers.
