## What is the "low cortisol elite" meme?

If you've spent time in health-optimization corners of the internet, you've probably seen it: the "low cortisol elite" trend.

The idea is simple:

- High performers supposedly have naturally low cortisol.
- They stay calm under pressure. They never stress.
- If you can lower your cortisol enough, you'll join them.

It sounds convincing. **But when you look at the actual science, this story falls apart fast.**

## What does cortisol actually do?

Cortisol is your body's main stress hormone. Your adrenal glands release it in response to physical or mental stress. But stress isn't its only job.

Cortisol also:

- Helps control blood sugar and metabolism
- Controls inflammation throughout your body
- Plays a part in forming memories
- Follows your sleep-wake cycle
- Supports your heart and blood vessels
- Helps keep your blood pressure up

### It follows a daily rhythm

Healthy cortisol peaks about 30 minutes after you wake up. This is called the **cortisol awakening response**. Then it slowly falls through the day, reaching its lowest point around the middle of the night.

This pattern matters. You need that morning rise to get you moving. You need the evening dip to sleep well.

**The rhythm matters just as much as the amount.**

## Why is "low is better" wrong?

**The meme assumes lower cortisol always means better performance. Biology doesn't work that way.**

Cortisol that is low all the time causes real problems. People with adrenal insufficiency (when the body can't make enough cortisol) have severe tiredness, low blood pressure, weight loss and muscle weakness.

Truly low cortisol points to Addison's disease or adrenal insufficiency. That's a real but rare and serious medical condition that needs treatment ([StatPearls, *Physiology, Cortisol*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)).

Some research has also linked blunted (weak) cortisol responses with conditions like chronic fatigue syndrome and PTSD. That's not a picture of high performance.

Meanwhile, **short cortisol rises are part of normal performance.** Your body releases cortisol during exercise because you need it to free up energy and manage inflammation.

The goal isn't to get rid of cortisol. It's to have a healthy pattern that fits what you're actually doing. See what healthy patterns look like on [cortisol levels](/cortisol/levels/).

## What do elite performers actually have?

**There's no good evidence that they simply have low cortisol.**

A healthy stress system is meant to rise when you face a challenge and settle when it passes. That's true for everyone, not just athletes or soldiers.

The real sign of resilience isn't low cortisol. It's being able to **respond to a challenge and then return to normal**. That takes a working stress system, not a switched-off one.

## Where does the meme come from?

So why does the "low cortisol elite" idea feel true to so many people? Three reasons.

**1. Mixing up short-term and long-term stress.** Cortisol that stays high for a long time does cause problems. It's linked to weight gain, sleep problems, anxiety and a weaker immune system. But the fix isn't to shut cortisol down. It's to reduce ongoing stress and recover better.

**2. Mixing up cause and effect.** Some wellness influencers notice that burned-out people have disrupted cortisol patterns (which can include weak responses). They then assume that lowering cortisol will prevent burnout. But the disrupted patterns may be a **result** of long-term strain, not the cause.

This is the same mix-up behind the popular "adrenal fatigue" label. A systematic review found it is not a supported medical diagnosis ([Cadegiani & Kater, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4997656/)).

**3. Simple stories sell.** "Lower your cortisol" sells supplements and courses. "Improve your cortisol rhythm through better sleep, recovery and stress management" is accurate, but less catchy.

## What actually works?

If you want a healthier cortisol pattern, focus on the basics that research supports:

- **A steady sleep schedule.** Going to bed and waking up at the same times supports your body clock, which times your cortisol rhythm.
- **Morning light.** Bright light early in the day helps set your body clock.
- **Regular exercise.** Choose activity that matches how well you recover.
- **Enough recovery.** Rest days, sleep and stress management give your system time to settle.
- **Steady blood sugar.** Regular meals with protein, and avoiding long fasts, help you avoid big dips.

**None of these are about pushing cortisol down.** They're about supporting a natural, healthy pattern.

## How can I check my cortisol pattern?

Only a lab test measures cortisol. If you're curious or worried, a doctor can decide whether testing makes sense for you, and which test. See [how cortisol testing works](/cortisol/test/).
