Purple stretch marks do not automatically mean high cortisol. Their color, location or appearance in a photo cannot establish a hormone diagnosis.

## Why marks appear

The [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/cosmetic/scars-stretch-marks/stretch-marks-why-appear) describes stretch marks as scars associated with rapid skin stretching or shrinking. They can occur with puberty, pregnancy, weight changes or rapid muscle growth. Prolonged topical corticosteroid use and some medical conditions can also be relevant.

New marks may be pink, red, purple or brown, depending on skin color; their appearance often changes with time. Color alone therefore does not separate ordinary marks from a medical cause.

## When the surrounding history matters

If marks are new and unexplained, or accompanied by other changes that concern you, discuss the whole picture with a clinician. Note when they appeared, recent body changes and medicines, including steroid creams. A cortisol questionnaire cannot decide whether testing is needed.

Avoid two opposite shortcuts: assuming every purple mark is a hormone disorder, or assuming a person is healthy because their marks do not match a checklist.

## What treatment can promise

AAD notes that treatments may make stretch marks less noticeable but do not reliably erase them. There is no single treatment that works for everyone. Ask about options suited to your skin and circumstances rather than using a cortisol-lowering routine as a scar treatment.
