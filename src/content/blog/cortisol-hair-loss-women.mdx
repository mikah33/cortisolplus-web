Seeing more hair in the shower drain? The cause often happened months ago.

The link between stress and hair loss isn't instant. There's a frustrating delay, which makes it hard to work out what went wrong.

Knowing the timeline helps you connect a stressful time with thinning hair that shows up later. Here's what the science says about cortisol, hair growth cycles, and why stress from three months ago can show up now.

## How does cortisol affect hair?

**Cortisol is your body's main stress hormone** ([StatPearls: Physiology, Cortisol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)). When it stays high, it affects tissues all over your body, including your scalp.

Hair follicles (the tiny pockets hair grows from) have receptors for cortisol. Research suggests ongoing stress can:

- Shorten the hair's growing phase
- Push follicles into the resting phase too early

**The problem is usually a big or long-lasting stress.** It's not the normal spikes from a hard workout or one stressful day. When stress goes on for weeks or months, more follicles may stop growing early.

Women often go through this during long stretches of stress, like losing a job, divorce, illness or big life changes.

Perimenopause can also bring hair changes, as other hormone levels shift.

## Why does hair fall out 3 months later?

**Hair doesn't fall out the moment stress hits.** It follows a cycle with three phases:

At any time, most of your hair (about 85 to 90%) is growing. Only 10 to 15% is resting.

After a big stress, more follicles can switch from growing to resting. This is called **telogen effluvium**.

The catch? Those hairs don't fall out right away. They sit in the resting phase for about three months before they shed ([telogen effluvium: a comprehensive review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6709511/)).

So hair loss you notice in June may come from stress in March. That's why it helps to think back a few months to what was going on before the shedding began.

## What can trigger stress hair loss in women?

**A big physical or emotional stress** can affect hair. For example:

- Surgery or a hospital stay
- Serious illness or infection
- Losing weight fast or strict dieting
- After giving birth (cortisol and other hormones shift a lot)
- Not getting enough sleep for a long time
- Long work stress or burnout
- Emotional trauma or grief

Physical stresses often have a clearer timeline than emotional ones. For example, a woman might notice more shedding about three months after surgery.

With ongoing work stress, the pattern can be harder to see. The stress goes on without a clear start date.

**Other things can cause hair loss that looks the same.** These include changes to hormonal birth control, thyroid problems and low iron. That's why working with a healthcare provider matters. Stress is one piece of a bigger puzzle.

## What can I do about it?

**The good news: stress shedding (telogen effluvium) is usually reversible.** Once the stress passes, follicles usually go back to their normal cycle.

Recovery takes time, because of the same cycle that caused the delay. You might not see improvement for **3 to 6 months** after the stress eases. New hair has to go through its phases.

Steps that help:

- **Deal with the cause of stress.** If you can, reduce or remove what's causing the stress. See these [practical ways to lower cortisol](/cortisol/lower/) for approaches backed by evidence.
- **Put sleep first.** Sleep and cortisol affect each other. Poor sleep can raise cortisol, and high cortisol can disrupt sleep.
- **Eat enough protein.** Hair is made of protein. Strict diets can make shedding worse.
- **Be patient with your scalp.** Harsh treatments or constant checking won't speed recovery, and may add stress.

## How can I track my trend over time?

**Patterns matter more than one-off numbers.** The stress-hair link plays out over months. A single test shows one day, but hair loss reflects what your body went through over weeks.

Use the [free stress and sleep journal](/resources/stress-sleep-journal/) to write down what you notice, in your own words.

A symptom quiz can't estimate your cortisol levels. It also can't tell you whether you need a hormone test.
