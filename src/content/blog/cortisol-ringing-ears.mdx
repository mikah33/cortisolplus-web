**Yes, stress and cortisol can play a part in tinnitus.** Stress can make ringing in your ears more noticeable, and harder to live with.

Cortisol is a hormone made by your adrenal glands. It plays a role in your body's stress response ([MedlinePlus: Cortisol test](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003693.htm)).

Doctors don't say cortisol itself causes tinnitus. **It's stress as a whole that can trigger or worsen it.** The NHS lists stress and anxiety among the things linked to tinnitus ([NHS: Tinnitus](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/)).

## What is tinnitus?

**Tinnitus means hearing a sound that doesn't come from outside you.** Other people can't hear it ([NIDCD: Tinnitus](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/tinnitus)).

It can sound like ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/)):

- Ringing or buzzing
- Whooshing or humming
- Hissing or throbbing
- Even music

You might hear it in one ear or both. It can come and go, or be there all the time.

The NHS says tinnitus is **usually not a sign of anything serious**, and it may get better on its own.

## How does stress affect tinnitus?

**Stress and tinnitus can feed each other in a loop.**

Tinnitus UK, a tinnitus charity, explains that ([Tinnitus UK: Tinnitus and stress](https://tinnitus.org.uk/understanding-tinnitus/living-with-tinnitus/tinnitus-and-stress/)):

- It's common for tinnitus to **start at a time of high stress**, or after a stressful stretch.
- Existing tinnitus often **gets worse during stressful times**.
- That can add more stress, which makes the tinnitus feel worse again. This is the **stress–tinnitus cycle**.

### Why does it seem louder when I'm stressed?

**Your attention locks onto it.** If you see your tinnitus as a threat, your brain keeps checking on it. That focus can make it seem much louder and harder to ignore ([Tinnitus UK](https://tinnitus.org.uk/understanding-tinnitus/living-with-tinnitus/tinnitus-and-stress/)).

The good news: because attention plays a part, **easing stress and worry can make tinnitus easier to cope with**.

## What else causes ringing in the ears?

**Stress is often only part of the picture.** Other common causes include ([NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/tinnitus); [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/)):

- **Loud noise**, at work, at concerts or from other loud sounds
- **Hearing loss**, often linked to age or noise
- **Earwax** or an ear infection blocking the ear
- **Some medicines**, such as certain antibiotics, aspirin and anti-inflammatory painkillers like ibuprofen
- **Head or neck injuries**
- Other health conditions, such as Ménière's disease, diabetes or thyroid problems

That's why it's worth getting tinnitus checked if it keeps bothering you. Don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own. Talk to your doctor first.

## What can I do to cope?

**Many simple steps can help you live with tinnitus.** The NHS suggests ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/)):

1. **Relax.** Deep breathing or yoga may help.
2. **Sleep better.** Stick to a bedtime routine and cut down on caffeine.
3. **Avoid things that make it worse**, such as stress or loud background noise.
4. **Don't sit in total silence.** Soft music or sounds (called sound therapy) may distract you from the tinnitus.
5. **Don't focus on it.** Hobbies and activities can take your mind off it.
6. **Join a support group.** Talking to other people with tinnitus may help you cope.

Tinnitus UK also suggests daily relaxation, exercise and time with other people to lower overall stress ([Tinnitus UK](https://tinnitus.org.uk/understanding-tinnitus/living-with-tinnitus/tinnitus-and-stress/)).

For a simple place to start, try one of these [breathing exercises](/cortisol/lower/breathing-exercises/).

### What treatments are there?

**A doctor or hearing specialist can help.** Options include ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/); [NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/tinnitus)):

- **Hearing aids**, if you also have hearing loss
- **Sound therapy** devices or apps
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**, a talking therapy that changes how you react to the sound
- **Tinnitus counselling** or tinnitus retraining therapy

## When should I see a doctor?

**See a GP or doctor if** your tinnitus ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/)):

- Happens often or is always there
- Is getting worse
- Is bothering you, for example affecting your sleep or focus, or making you feel anxious or low

**Get medical help promptly if** ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/); [Merck Manual](https://www.merckmanuals.com/home/ear-nose-and-throat-disorders/symptoms-of-ear-disorders/ear-ringing-or-buzzing); [Cleveland Clinic: Pulsatile tinnitus](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/23422-pulsatile-tinnitus)):

- You have **tinnitus in one ear only**
- It's **pulsing**, or beats in time with your heartbeat. The NHS says ask for an urgent GP appointment.
- It comes with **dizziness** or trouble with balance, walking, seeing or speaking

**Get emergency help (A&E or 999 in the UK, or your local emergency number) if** you have tinnitus ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tinnitus/)):

- With **sudden hearing loss**
- With **weakness in the muscles of your face**
- With a **spinning feeling (vertigo)**
- After a **head injury**

**Sudden hearing loss is urgent.** If you lose hearing suddenly, or over a few days, treat it as a medical emergency and see a doctor straight away ([NIDCD: Sudden deafness](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness)). Getting treated quickly gives you the best chance of getting some hearing back.
