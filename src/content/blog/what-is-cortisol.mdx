## What is cortisol, in one sentence?

**Cortisol is a hormone that gives you energy and focus when your body needs it.**

Your adrenal glands release it on a daily rhythm. They also release it on demand, whenever your brain decides something matters.

The internet calls it "the stress hormone", like a villain. It isn't. It's a survival hormone that happens to rise during stress. You'd die without it.

**Cortisol itself is never the problem.** The problem is cortisol staying high when it should be coming back down.

## Where does cortisol come from?

It's made by your **adrenal glands**. These are two small, triangle-shaped caps that sit on top of your kidneys.

The chain of command that releases it is called the **HPA axis** (hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal). It works in three steps:

1. Your **hypothalamus** (in your brain) decides something is stressful. That could be a noise, a missed deadline, low blood sugar or a hard workout.
2. It tells your **pituitary** (also in your brain) to release a messenger hormone called ACTH.
3. ACTH travels through your blood to the **adrenals**, which then release cortisol.

From trigger to peak blood cortisol takes about 15 minutes.

The loop also has a built-in off switch. Cortisol signals back to the hypothalamus to stop making more. In a healthy system, you get a clean rise and fall.

## What does cortisol actually do?

When it's doing its job, cortisol:

- **Frees up sugar for fuel.** It pulls sugar (glucose) from your liver into your blood, so your muscles and brain have energy.
- **Sharpens your attention.** The morning cortisol pulse is what really wakes you up, not coffee.
- **Pauses non-urgent jobs.** Digestion, reproduction and slow tissue repair get turned down while cortisol is high. Useful in a sprint. Bad in a marathon.
- **Calms inflammation.** Man-made versions of cortisol (hydrocortisone, prednisone) are prescribed for exactly this reason.
- **Keeps your blood pressure up.** Together with another hormone, aldosterone, it helps you stand up without fainting.

Take all that away, as happens in Addison's disease (when the adrenals fail), and people become exhausted and dizzy. They get low blood pressure and can have dangerously low blood sugar ([StatPearls, *Physiology, Cortisol*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)).

Cortisol is the hormone that lets you function.

## Does cortisol change through the day?

**Yes. It follows a strong daily curve.**

- **30 to 45 minutes after waking:** a sharp rise called the **cortisol awakening response (CAR)**. This is what pulls you out of morning grogginess.
- **Mid-morning:** the peak.
- **Afternoon:** a steady fall.
- **Evening:** low.
- **Around midnight:** the lowest point. Growth hormone and melatonin take over, and your body switches to repair.

A healthy pattern is a tall morning peak that comes down smoothly through the day.

Two patterns are usually a problem:

- **Flat curve:** a low morning peak that never really lifts. Common with long-term stress and burnout.
- **Upside-down curve:** low in the morning, high at night. This wrecks sleep and recovery.

See what normal and abnormal look like on the [cortisol levels page](/cortisol/levels/).

## What makes cortisol spike?

**Anything your brain reads as "this matters".** Some everyday triggers:

- **Sudden stress:** a stressful email, a near-miss while driving, an argument.
- **Hard exercise:** especially long endurance or high-intensity sessions. This is healthy and short-lived if you recover afterward.
- **Low blood sugar:** skipped meals, long fasts, or a crash after a very sugary meal.
- **Caffeine:** raises cortisol a little, especially on an empty stomach ([Lovallo et al., 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2257922/)).
- **Poor sleep:** even one short night raises the next day's cortisol curve ([Leproult et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865)).
- **Alcohol:** especially heavy evening drinking. It raises cortisol overnight and flattens the morning peak.

None of these is a problem on its own. Cortisol is *designed* to spike. What matters is what happens next.

## Why is "high cortisol" the wrong question?

The real question is rarely *"Is my cortisol high right now?"* It's **"Does my cortisol come back down?"**

- **Healthy:** trigger, then spike, then it settles back to normal within an hour or two.
- **Not healthy:** trigger, then spike, then it stays up for hours. Another small trigger keeps it up. This repeats for weeks, until your normal level itself creeps higher.

Cortisol that stays high for a long time is the real problem. It tends to look like this:

- Wired-but-tired energy, especially in the evening
- Belly weight gain that resists diet
- Sleep that doesn't leave you rested
- Flat or anxious mood without an obvious cause
- Cravings for sugar and salt
- Getting sick often, because cortisol holds back your immune system

See the full list and what each sign means on the [cortisol symptoms page](/cortisol/symptoms/).

## How can you actually tell?

You have three options.

1. **A lab cortisol test.** This can use blood, saliva or urine. Which test, when to collect it, and how to read it all depend on the medical question. There is no single four-point test that suits everyone. See [how to test cortisol](/cortisol/test/).
2. **A symptom checklist.** Useful as a first look, but poor for diagnosis. Most long-term cortisol symptoms overlap with thyroid problems, anemia and depression.
3. **Body signals from a wearable.** Heart rate variability (HRV), resting heart rate, sleep and wrist temperature give useful context. But they don't show your cortisol curve. [Cortisol+](/how-it-works/) combines available signals into a wellness score. It has not been shown to be accurate against cortisol tests.

Lab tests and wearable data answer different questions. **Wearable history is not a replacement for a cortisol test chosen by a clinician.**

## What helps, and what doesn't?

These have real evidence for lowering long-term cortisol:

- **[Sleep](/cortisol/lower/sleep/):** by far the biggest lever. One extra hour of sleep changes cortisol more than any supplement.
- **[Breathing exercises](/cortisol/lower/breathing-exercises/):** slow breathing through your nose lowers cortisol within minutes.
- **[Zone 2 cardio](/cortisol/lower/exercise/):** easy, steady cardio improves HRV and helps the HPA axis recover. Too much hard interval training (HIIT) does the opposite.
- **[Magnesium glycinate and ashwagandha](/cortisol/lower/supplements/):** the two supplements with the strongest published cortisol evidence.
- **Morning sunlight:** ties your cortisol curve to your body clock. Ten minutes is enough.
- **Cutting evening alcohol:** usually the change with the biggest payoff.

What doesn't work, despite the marketing:

- Adrenal "support" supplements with secret blends and no stated dose of ashwagandha
- The cortisol mocktail ([covered here](/blog/cortisol-mocktail-recipe/))
- Anything claiming to "reset" your adrenals in a week
