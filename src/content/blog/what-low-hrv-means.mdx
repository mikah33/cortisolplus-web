## What does low HRV mean?

Your fitness tracker says your [HRV](/hrv/) is low. Again. But what does that actually mean?

**Low HRV that stays low is a signal about your nervous system, and it can help you spot long-term stress early.**

Heart rate variability (HRV) measures the gaps in time between your heartbeats. When those gaps stay small and regular, your body is telling you something about the state of your nervous system.

Here's what the science shows about low HRV and stress.

## How does HRV show what my nervous system is doing?

Your heart doesn't beat like a metronome. It speeds up and slows down a little with each breath, and in response to everything going on in your body.

HRV measures these tiny timing changes between beats.

- **Higher HRV** means your autonomic nervous system (the part that runs things like your heartbeat on its own) is flexible and responsive.
- **Lower HRV** suggests it's stuck in one mode.

### The two sides of your nervous system

- **Parasympathetic ("rest and digest"):** active when you're relaxed, eating or recovering. It raises HRV.
- **Sympathetic ("fight or flight"):** active during stress, exercise or danger. It lowers HRV.

When you're healthy and not stressed, the two balance each other out. At rest, the "rest and digest" side leads, which gives higher HRV.

When you're stressed for a long time, the "fight or flight" side stays switched on. That keeps HRV low.

## How is low HRV linked to cortisol?

**Low HRV often goes along with high [cortisol](/cortisol/), your main stress hormone. But it can't tell you your exact cortisol level.**

Low HRV doesn't just reflect stress in the moment. Research shows that long-term mental stress raises cortisol and lowers HRV.

Both move together because they're controlled by overlapping parts of the brain, especially the hypothalamus and brainstem.

### What happens under ongoing stress

When your brain senses ongoing stress, it sets off both cortisol release and the "fight or flight" system. That stress could be work pressure, poor sleep, overtraining or emotional strain.

The cortisol response can last hours or days. The HRV drop can last just as long.

### Signs that often show up together

This is why low HRV often appears alongside other signs of high cortisol:

- Trouble falling or staying asleep
- Feeling tired but wired
- Brain fog, or trouble concentrating
- Slower recovery from workouts
- Getting minor illnesses more often

Low HRV won't tell you your exact cortisol level. But it gives you a real-time view of the body stress that cortisol is also reacting to.

## What causes HRV to stay low?

**A single low reading means almost nothing.** Your HRV drops naturally during and after exercise, when you're sick, after drinking alcohol and when you haven't slept enough.

When your baseline (your usual level) stays low for weeks, that points to stress that keeps going:

- **Physical stress:** overtraining without enough recovery lowers HRV. Athletes who ignore low HRV trends often end up injured or burned out. Long-term illness and inflammation also lower HRV.
- **Mental stress:** work deadlines, relationship problems, money worries and caring for someone all lower HRV over time. Your body doesn't tell physical and mental stress apart. Both switch on the same systems.
- **Daily habits:** poor sleep is one of the biggest things that lowers HRV. Even one bad night drops HRV the next day. Weeks of poor sleep keep it low. A lot of caffeine, especially later in the day, can also lower HRV by keeping your "fight or flight" side switched on.
- **Age and fitness:** HRV naturally goes down with age. Aerobic fitness raises HRV, and not moving much lowers it. This is one reason regular exercise helps buffer stress.

## What can I do about low HRV?

**Don't obsess over the number. Work on what's behind it.**

- **Put sleep first.** Keep steady sleep and wake times, a cool dark bedroom, and fewer screens before bed. Sleep is when your "rest and digest" side leads and HRV recovers.
- **Ease off training if you need to.** More exercise isn't always better. If you're training hard and your HRV stays low for more than a few days, take a rest day or switch to easy movement.
- **Manage stress every day.** Slow breathing, meditation and yoga have all been shown to raise HRV over time. Even five minutes of slow breathing, at around six breaths per minute, boosts HRV for a while and trains your nervous system.
- **Check the basics.** Cut back on alcohol. Have less caffeine after noon. Eat regular meals with enough protein and carbohydrates to support recovery.
- **Watch the trend.** HRV bounces around from day to day. What matters is the weekly or monthly trend.

If your HRV stays low even after you've dealt with the obvious causes, talk to a healthcare provider about it.
