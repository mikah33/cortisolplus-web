## What does low HRV mean?

Your fitness tracker says your [HRV](/hrv/) is low. Again. But what does that actually mean?

**Low HRV that stays low is a signal about your nervous system, and it may help you spot long-term stress.**

Heart rate variability (HRV) measures the gaps in time between your heartbeats. When those gaps stay small and regular, your body is telling you something about the state of your nervous system.

Here's what the science shows about low HRV and stress.

## How does HRV show what my nervous system is doing?

Your heart doesn't beat like a metronome. It speeds up and slows down a little with each breath, and in response to everything going on in your body.

HRV measures these tiny timing changes between beats.

- **Higher HRV** means your autonomic nervous system (the part that runs things like your heartbeat on its own) is flexible and responsive.
- **Lower HRV** can suggest it's less flexible.

### The two sides of your nervous system

- **Parasympathetic ("rest and digest"):** active when you're relaxed, eating or recovering. It raises HRV.
- **Sympathetic ("fight or flight"):** active during stress, exercise or danger. It lowers HRV.

When you're healthy and not stressed, the two balance each other out. At rest, the "rest and digest" side leads, which gives higher HRV.

When you're stressed for a long time, the "fight or flight" side stays switched on. That keeps HRV low.

## Does low HRV mean high cortisol?

**Stress can affect both cortisol, your main stress hormone, and HRV. But HRV can't tell you your cortisol level.**

Cortisol and HRV are different signals, and they don't always move together. A low HRV reading doesn't mean your cortisol is high.

### What happens under ongoing stress

Ongoing stress can keep your "fight or flight" system switched on. That stress could be work pressure, poor sleep, overtraining or emotional strain.

HRV gives you a view of what your nervous system is doing. It isn't a hormone test. If you're worried about your hormones, a doctor can decide whether [cortisol testing](/cortisol/test/) makes sense, and which test.

## What causes HRV to stay low?

**A single low reading means almost nothing.** Your HRV drops naturally during and after exercise, when you're sick, after drinking alcohol and when you haven't slept enough.

When your baseline (your usual level) stays low for weeks, that points to stress that keeps going:

- **Physical stress:** overtraining without enough recovery can lower HRV. Long-term illness and inflammation can also lower HRV.
- **Mental stress:** work deadlines, relationship problems, money worries and caring for someone can all lower HRV over time. Physical and mental stress can switch on the same stress systems.
- **Daily habits:** poor sleep can lower HRV, sometimes as soon as the next day. A lot of caffeine may also lower HRV for some people.
- **Age and fitness:** HRV naturally goes down with age. Aerobic fitness raises HRV, and not moving much lowers it. This is one reason regular exercise helps buffer stress.

## What can I do about low HRV?

**Don't obsess over the number. Work on what's behind it.**

- **Put sleep first.** Keep steady sleep and wake times, a cool dark bedroom, and fewer screens before bed. Sleep is when your "rest and digest" side usually leads.
- **Ease off training if you need to.** More exercise isn't always better. If you're training hard and your HRV stays low for more than a few days, take a rest day or switch to easy movement.
- **Manage stress every day.** Slow breathing, meditation and yoga may help some people. Slow breathing, at around six breaths per minute, can raise HRV while you're doing it.
- **Check the basics.** Cut back on alcohol. Have less caffeine after noon. Eat regular meals.
- **Watch the trend.** HRV bounces around from day to day. What matters is the weekly or monthly trend.

If your HRV stays low even after you've dealt with the obvious causes, talk to a healthcare provider about it.
