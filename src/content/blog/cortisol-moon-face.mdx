Has your face looked rounder and fuller lately? You may have seen the term "moon face" online and wondered if cortisol is behind it.

**The short answer is yes, cortisol can play a part.** Moon face is a well-known sign of having too much cortisol, or medicines that act like it, for a long time.

Cortisol is your body's main stress hormone. This guide explains what moon face is, what causes it, and when to get it checked.

## What is moon face?

**Moon face is a round, full, puffy face.** Cleveland Clinic describes it as "swelling that builds up on the sides of your face, making it look puffy and round, like a full moon" ([Cleveland Clinic: Moon face](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)).

It happens when fat deposits build up along the sides of the face. Doctors may call it "moon facies". Some people online call it "cortisol face".

The good news: "Moon face usually isn't painful or dangerous" ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)). But it's often a sign of something worth knowing about.

## What causes moon face?

**Moon face is usually caused by steroid medicines or by the body making too much cortisol.**

### Steroid (glucocorticoid) medicines

Steroid medicines such as prednisone are some of the most common causes ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)). They are also called corticosteroids or glucocorticoids. Doctors prescribe them for things like autoimmune diseases, blood disorders, some cancers and inflammatory conditions.

Cleveland Clinic says anyone who takes corticosteroids "for a long time (more than a few weeks) has a high risk of developing moon face".

MedlinePlus agrees: the most common cause of Cushing syndrome is "taking too much glucocorticoid or corticosteroid medicine", such as prednisone, dexamethasone or prednisolone ([MedlinePlus: Cushing syndrome](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000410.htm)).

### Cushing's syndrome

**Cushing's syndrome happens when there's too much cortisol in the body for a long time.**

The most common cause is high doses of steroid medicine. Less often, a growth in the pituitary or adrenal glands makes the body produce too much cortisol ([NHS: Cushing's syndrome](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cushings-syndrome/)).

A "red, round face" is one of its main signs ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cushings-syndrome/)). MedlinePlus lists a "round, red, full face (moon face)" as a symptom most people with Cushing syndrome have.

### Other causes

An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) can also make the face swell. This happens when the thyroid doesn't make enough thyroid hormone, and fluid builds up in the face ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)).

## Is moon face the same as everyday puffiness?

**No. Moon face has a hormone cause and lasts while that cause is there.** It comes from fat and fluid building up because of steroid medicines, too much cortisol or an underactive thyroid ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)).

Everyday puffiness is different. It has many common causes, such as salty food, alcohol, poor sleep and allergies, and it tends to come and go.

We cover everyday puffiness, and what social media gets wrong about it, in our guide to [cortisol face](/cortisol/symptoms/cortisol-face/).

### Where does everyday stress fit in?

**Everyday stress can contribute to how your face looks, but in a gentler way.** Harvard Health explains that cortisol "increases appetite, so that people will want to eat more", and that chronic stress can cut into sleep and exercise ([Harvard Health: Understanding the stress response](https://www.harvardhealth.org/staying-healthy/understanding-the-stress-response)).

Over time, that can mean some weight gain, which can show in the face as well as the body. That isn't the same as moon face, which comes from much higher cortisol levels or steroid medicines.

For fullness under the chin, see [cortisol double chin](/blog/cortisol-double-chin/).

## What signs mean I should see a doctor?

**Moon face rarely comes alone when cortisol is the cause.** The NHS lists other signs of Cushing's syndrome, including:

- more body fat on your neck, upper back, chest and tummy
- thin arms and legs
- bruising easily
- weak muscles
- mood changes
- irregular periods

([NHS: Cushing's syndrome](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cushings-syndrome/))

The NHS says to "see a GP if you have symptoms of Cushing's syndrome, particularly if you're taking steroid medicine".

A doctor can decide whether [cortisol testing](/cortisol/test/) makes sense, and which test to use.

## I take steroids. What should I do?

**Never stop a prescribed steroid on your own.** The NHS says, "Do not stop taking your steroid medicine without talking to your doctor first" ([NHS: Steroids](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/steroids/)).

If you've taken steroids for a while or at a high dose, the dose usually needs to come down slowly ([NHS: Steroids](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/steroids/)). Stopping suddenly can cause tiredness, weakness and other symptoms ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)).

If moon face is bothering you, talk to your doctor. They may be able to adjust your dose or suggest another option ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)).

## Does moon face go away?

**Usually, yes, as the cause is treated.** Cleveland Clinic says "moon face should disappear as you go through treatment" ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/moon-face)).

That might mean a lower steroid dose, a different medicine, or treatment for Cushing's syndrome or an underactive thyroid. Your doctor will guide you.
