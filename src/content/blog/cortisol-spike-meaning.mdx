## Why is everyone talking about cortisol spikes?

Scroll social media and you've probably seen someone warning about a "cortisol spike". Maybe it was your morning coffee. Or skipping breakfast. Or checking your phone before bed.

The term gets blamed for weight gain, anxiety, skin problems and basically everything else.

So what is a cortisol spike, really? And does the science back up the panic?

**This guide is about what the term means**, and when a spike actually matters. Looking for the specific triggers instead? See [the 12 things that cause a cortisol spike](/blog/cortisol-spike-causes/).

## Is a cortisol spike bad?

**Usually not. In real biology, cortisol spikes are normal.**

Online, "cortisol spike" usually means any moment your [cortisol](/cortisol/) goes up. Influencers treat it like a red alert, something to avoid at all costs.

But your body is meant to release cortisol in certain situations. That's how the hormone works. Cortisol:

- Raises blood sugar for quick energy
- Calms inflammation in the short term
- Helps you wake up in the morning
- Kicks in during physical or mental stress

You get a natural cortisol spike every morning. It's called the **cortisol awakening response**, and it happens about 30 minutes after you wake up. This isn't damage. It's your body getting you ready for the day.

The problem isn't the spike. It's when cortisol **stays high too long**, or rises at the wrong times again and again.

## When does a spike actually matter?

**When it becomes chronic, meaning it keeps happening for a long time.**

Not all cortisol rises are equal. A short spike during a workout is expected and healthy. Constant high cortisol from ongoing stress is different.

Research shows repeated or long-lasting high cortisol can affect your health:

- **Sleep:** high evening cortisol can make it harder to fall asleep.
- **Blood sugar:** constant high levels may add to insulin resistance over time.
- **Immune system:** long-term high cortisol can weaken your immune response.
- **Mood:** long-term high levels are linked to anxiety and depression symptoms.

A single spike from an argument or a coffee won't wreck your health. **It's the pattern that matters.**

Your body is built to handle short-term stress. It isn't built for weeks or months of pressure with no recovery.

## What causes a spike that matters?

Some triggers are backed by evidence. Others are social media myths. Here's the short version. For the full, ranked list, see [12 things that cause a cortisol spike](/blog/cortisol-spike-causes/).

**Real triggers:**

- Hard exercise (short-lived and usually good for you)
- Not enough sleep, or poor sleep
- Mental stress, especially stress that is ongoing or unpredictable
- Low blood sugar or long fasts, in some people
- Long-term inflammation or illness

**Overblown or misunderstood:**

- **A single cup of coffee.** Caffeine can raise cortisol slightly, but the effect is small, and your body adapts if you drink it regularly.
- **Eating carbs.** In fact, very low-carb diets may raise cortisol more than balanced meals.
- **Looking at screens.** Stress from what you see matters more than the light itself.

Context decides whether a cortisol rise helps or harms. Sprinting away from danger? That's useful. Lying awake every night worrying about work emails? That's your stress system switched on with no break.

## What should I actually watch for?

**Instead of worrying about every possible "spike", look for signs that your stress response isn't recovering.**

Signs your cortisol pattern might be off:

- Waking up tired even after a full night's sleep
- Energy crashes in the late afternoon
- Trouble falling asleep even though you feel exhausted
- Getting sick more often than usual
- Weight gain around your middle that won't budge
- Feeling wired and tired at the same time

These signs **don't prove high cortisol**. They just suggest your stress and recovery might need attention.

Blood or saliva tests can measure your real cortisol levels through the day, if you and your doctor want hard data.

The real cost isn't one spike. It's ignoring the signs that your body never gets a break.
