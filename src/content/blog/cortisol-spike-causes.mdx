## What is cortisol, quickly?

Cortisol is your body's main stress hormone. Your adrenal glands release it on a daily rhythm. They also release it on demand through the HPA axis, your brain-to-adrenal stress system ([StatPearls, *Physiology, Cortisol*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)).

It helps you wake up, react to danger and manage your metabolism. But too much cortisol at the wrong times can upset your sleep, mood and health.

Knowing what causes a cortisol spike in your day helps you make better choices about stress.

**This page is the trigger checklist.** Want to know what a cortisol spike actually is, and when one is normal? Start with [what is a cortisol spike?](/blog/cortisol-spike-meaning/)

## The 12 big cortisol triggers

### 1. Poor sleep or not enough sleep

**Even one bad night raises cortisol the next day.** Sleep loss raises the following evening's cortisol levels ([Leproult et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865)).

Your body sees sleep loss as a threat, so it makes more cortisol to keep you alert.

Long-term sleep problems can become a loop. Stress can make it harder to fall asleep, and short sleep can raise cortisol again.

### 2. Hard or very long exercise

Exercise is healthy, but hard workouts raise cortisol for a while. That's normal. Your body needs extra energy during physical stress.

The problem comes when you overtrain without enough recovery. Athletes who push too hard without rest can end up with cortisol that stays high.

### 3. Caffeine

Coffee and energy drinks can trigger cortisol release ([Lovallo et al., 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2257922/)).

The effect is stronger in people who don't usually drink caffeine. If you're already stressed, caffeine can make your cortisol response bigger.

### 4. Mental stress

Work deadlines, relationship fights and money worries are some of the most common causes of cortisol spikes.

Your brain doesn't tell the difference between physical danger and an angry email. **Both switch on the same stress system** ([StatPearls, *Physiology, Stress Reaction*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK541120/)).

### 5. Skipping meals or low blood sugar

When your blood sugar drops too low, cortisol rises to release stored sugar (glucose).

That's why you might feel shaky, grumpy or anxious when you haven't eaten. Regular meals help you avoid those dips.

### 6. Inflammation and illness

Infections, injuries and long-term inflammatory conditions all trigger cortisol release. Your immune system uses cortisol to keep inflammation in check.

That's why you might notice [symptoms of high cortisol](/cortisol/symptoms/) when you're fighting a cold or dealing with ongoing health problems.

### 7. Long-term pain

Pain that doesn't go away keeps your stress system switched on.

Some people with chronic pain show changed cortisol patterns through the day.

### 8. Alcohol

Alcohol might make you feel relaxed at first, but it upsets your normal cortisol rhythm.

Drinking, especially in the evening, can disturb your sleep. Poor sleep is itself a cortisol trigger.

### 9. Very hot or very cold temperatures

Both heat and cold stress your body. Your body releases cortisol as it works to keep its core temperature steady.

This is usually brief, unless you're exposed for a long time.

### 10. Not drinking enough water

Dehydration is a physical stress, especially during exercise or hot weather. The link with cortisol is less clear than for the triggers above.

Staying hydrated is still an easy habit to keep.

### 11. Social stress and conflict

Arguments, public speaking, being left out or feeling judged all switch on your stress response.

Social threats set off the same hormone chain as physical threats. For some people, social stress causes bigger cortisol spikes than other kinds of stress.

### 12. Shift work and body-clock disruption

Your cortisol follows a daily rhythm. It's highest in the morning and lowest at night.

Night shifts and travel across time zones throw this off. When your body clock is confused, cortisol can spike at the wrong times.

## How do I know my cortisol is spiking?

**A sudden cortisol spike often comes with feelings you can notice:**

- A fast heartbeat
- Feeling jittery or on edge
- Sudden alertness, or finding it hard to relax
- Changes in your digestion
- Tense muscles

These overlap with general anxiety. That makes sense, because cortisol is part of your anxiety response.

The difference is that cortisol spikes are tied to specific triggers. They usually fade when the stressful thing passes.

If you notice these feelings again and again, it's worth working out which triggers apply to you. Some people have several spikes a day without spotting the pattern.

## What can I do about it?

Once you know your own triggers, you can take steps to [lower cortisol](/cortisol/lower/) naturally. Start with these:

- Protect your sleep schedule
- Watch when and how much caffeine you have
- Eat regular meals with some protein
- Build recovery time into your exercise
- Find stress management techniques that work for you

Small, steady changes are easier to keep up. **Keep at it steadily. It doesn't have to be perfect.**

## Can a checklist tell me my risk?

Use the [free stress and sleep journal](/resources/stress-sleep-journal/) to note what you notice in your own words.

A symptom questionnaire **can't estimate your cortisol levels**. It also can't decide whether you need a hormone test.
