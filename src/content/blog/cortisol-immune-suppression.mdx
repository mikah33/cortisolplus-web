Stress, cortisol and immunity are linked topics in research.

But **"my score is high, so my immune system is weak" is not something a watch can tell you.**

## Does stress research apply to me?

**Research findings are not a personal immune test.**

A [2012 study by Cohen and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3341031/) looked at two groups of volunteers. It studied stressful life events, glucocorticoid receptor resistance (when the body responds less to cortisol's signal) and how people reacted when they were deliberately exposed to a cold virus.

Its findings are about how the immune system is controlled, in a research setting. They don't create a watch score that predicts whether one person will get sick.

The immune system is more complex than cortisol just switching it off. That's why a study's results shouldn't become a checklist for diagnosing "cortisol immune suppression."

## What about medicines and hormone conditions?

**These need to be looked at separately.**

Glucocorticoid medicines (steroid medicines that act like cortisol) are used because they affect inflammation and the immune system. This includes transplant care.

Having too much cortisol for a long time also has medical effects. [NIDDK describes these situations](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/cushings-syndrome).

Neither one is the same as feeling stressed after a busy week.

**If you take a medicine that affects your immune system, follow your prescribing team's advice** about avoiding infections and when to contact them. A wellness app can't change that plan.

## What should I bring to my appointment?

**Bring your actual history, not a label.**

For repeated or worrying infections, write down:

- Each episode
- Your symptoms
- Any diagnoses
- Your medicines

Share this with your clinician instead of calling it a hormone imbalance. If your sleep or stress has also changed, mention that as background.
