Stress, cortisol and immunity are connected research topics, but “my score is high, so my immune system is suppressed” is not a conclusion a wearable can support.

## Stress research is not an individual immune test

A [2012 study by Cohen and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3341031/) examined stressful experiences, glucocorticoid receptor resistance and responses to experimental cold-virus exposure in two groups of volunteers. Its findings concern immune regulation in a research setting. They do not establish a wearable score that predicts whether a particular person will get sick.

Immune regulation is more complex than cortisol simply switching immunity off. That is why results from a study should not become a symptom checklist for diagnosing “cortisol immune suppression.”

## Medicines and endocrine disease need separate consideration

Glucocorticoid medicines are used for their effects on inflammation and the immune system, including in transplant care. Long-term cortisol excess also has clinical consequences. [NIDDK describes these contexts](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/cushings-syndrome); neither is interchangeable with feeling stressed after a busy week.

If you take an immune-affecting medicine, follow your prescribing team's instructions about infection precautions and when to contact them. A wellness app cannot adjust that plan.

## A useful record for your appointment

For recurrent or concerning infections, record the episodes, symptoms, any diagnoses and medicines. Bring the actual history to your clinician instead of labeling it a hormone imbalance. If sleep or stress has also changed, mention that as context.

Cortisol+ does not measure immune cells, inflammation, immune competence or infection risk. Its [wellness score](/how-it-works/) cannot prove that a supplement restored immunity or that a lower score means an infection has resolved.
