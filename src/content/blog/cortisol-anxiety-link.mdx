Cortisol and anxiety feed into each other in ways most people don't realize.

High cortisol can make you feel more anxious. Anxiety can make your cortisol jump. **Together they make a loop that's hard to get out of** until you understand what's going on in your body.

This link isn't "just in your head". It's real, measurable biology that affects how you feel, think and get through the day.

## How can cortisol cause anxiety?

**When cortisol stays high for too long, it changes how your brain works.**

Cortisol is your body's main stress hormone. Here is what too much of it can do:

- **It makes your "alarm" part of the brain louder.** The amygdala is the part of your brain that handles fear and threat. Too much cortisol makes it overactive, so more situations feel threatening, even when they're not.
- **It quiets your "calm, thinking" part of the brain.** Cortisol can turn down the prefrontal cortex, which handles clear thinking and emotional control. With less help from it, calming yourself down or thinking anxious thoughts through gets harder.

High cortisol also causes physical symptoms:

- Fast heartbeat
- Shallow breathing
- Tense muscles
- Stomach and digestion problems
- Sleep problems

These body feelings can set off more anxiety on their own. Your brain reads them as signs of danger, and you worry even more.

## How can anxiety raise cortisol?

**The loop runs both ways.**

When you feel anxious, your body switches on the HPA axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis). This system releases cortisol as part of your stress response ([StatPearls, *Physiology, Cortisol*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)).

Even stress that's only in your thoughts releases real cortisol. Worrying about work deadlines, arguments or money uses the same pathway as running from a predator.

Long-term anxiety means the HPA axis is switched on for a long time. Your body keeps releasing cortisol because your brain keeps signaling a threat. Over time, this can throw off your whole cortisol rhythm.

A healthy cortisol pattern is highest in the morning and drops at night. Long-term anxiety can flatten this curve. You may end up with high cortisol at the wrong times, or not enough when you need it.

## Why does the loop get stuck?

**Once the cycle starts, a few things keep it going:**

1. **Your stress system gets more sensitive.** Each time it switches on, the next time gets easier. Over time, it takes less to set it off.
2. **Sleep gets worse.** High night-time cortisol makes it hard to fall or stay asleep. Poor sleep raises the next day's cortisol and makes you more emotionally reactive. More reactivity means more anxiety and more cortisol.
3. **Avoiding things keeps them scary.** When anxiety makes you avoid situations, you never learn that you can handle them. Your brain keeps treating them as real threats that need a cortisol response.
4. **Daily habits add up.** Caffeine raises cortisol. Skipping meals causes cortisol spikes. Not moving enough stops cortisol from following its natural rhythm. Each of these can make both sides of the loop stronger.

## How do I break the cycle?

**Because the loop runs both ways, you can break it from either side.**

- **Slow breathing.** Slow, controlled breathing switches on your "rest and digest" system (the parasympathetic nervous system). It signals safety to your brain and lowers cortisol production. [Breathing exercises](/cortisol/lower/breathing-exercises/) are one of the fastest ways to ease a wave of anxiety and lower cortisol in the moment.
- **Movement.** Physical activity helps regulate your cortisol rhythm. Moderate exercise raises cortisol for a short time, but it improves how the HPA axis works overall. Regular movement helps your stress response match real threats better.
- **Sleep habits.** Protecting your sleep protects your cortisol rhythm. That means steady sleep and wake times, a dark and cool bedroom, and less screen time before bed.
- **Changing how you see things.** When you change how you interpret a situation, you change whether your brain calls for cortisol. Therapy such as CBT (cognitive behavioral therapy) may help bring cortisol patterns back toward normal in some people with anxiety disorders, **though the evidence is still limited**.
- **People.** Supportive relationships reduce how strongly cortisol reacts to stress. Even short, positive moments with others can lower cortisol levels.

## How can I check my own pattern?

**Tracking shows what's really happening, not just what you assume.**

Our [cortisol calculator](/tools/cortisol-calculator/) helps you think through whether your symptoms and patterns suggest your cortisol is out of balance. It's a starting point for understanding your own situation.

Standard cortisol tests use saliva, blood or urine. They give you snapshots. These tests can confirm whether your cortisol levels or rhythm are off. They're especially useful if you're working with a healthcare provider on managing anxiety.
