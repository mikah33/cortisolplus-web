Waking sweaty at 2 a.m., 3 a.m. or 4 a.m. does not identify a cortisol problem. The time of an episode cannot distinguish a hormone condition from other causes.

## Warm bedding versus recurring night sweats

The [NHS describes night sweats](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/night-sweats/) as sweating that soaks clothing and bedding even in a cool sleeping environment. Possible causes include menopause, anxiety, medicines and other conditions; sometimes no cause is identified.

Regular sweats that wake you or worry you merit medical advice. The NHS also advises assessment when sweats occur with unexplained weight loss or symptoms such as a high temperature, cough or diarrhea.

## Record the episode without guessing its cause

Note whether you needed to change clothes or bedding, whether the room was hot, and any other symptoms. Add the names of medicines or supplements you use to the information you bring to the appointment. This gives your clinician a more useful starting point than an assumed “cortisol spike.”

A sleep journal can organize the dates, but do not wait to complete a week of tracking before seeking care for symptoms that concern you. Use the record as an aid, not a gatekeeper.

## What the Watch cannot establish

A Watch sleep record may help you recall an interrupted night. Wrist temperature and HRV are not measurements of cortisol and do not diagnose the cause of sweating. A lower Cortisol+ score the next day does not show that the underlying issue has resolved.
