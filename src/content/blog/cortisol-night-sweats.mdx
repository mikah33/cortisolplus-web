Waking up drenched in sweat at 3 AM is miserable. If it keeps happening, you might wonder what's wrong. One common culprit behind cortisol night sweats is the stress hormone cortisol throwing off your body's temperature control. When cortisol levels spike at the wrong times during sleep, your body's natural cooling system gets disrupted, leading to those soaked sheets.

## How cortisol affects body temperature

Your body temperature naturally drops when you fall asleep. This cooling effect is essential for deep, restorative sleep. Cortisol plays a direct role in this process because it influences your metabolism and heat production.

Normally, cortisol is lowest around midnight and starts rising in the early morning hours. This timing helps you wake up naturally. But when cortisol spikes during the night—whether from chronic stress, sleep disorders, or other causes—it can trigger your body's heat production at the wrong time.

High cortisol increases your metabolic rate. More metabolism means more heat. When your body suddenly generates extra heat while you're trying to sleep, your cooling mechanisms kick into overdrive. That's when the sweating starts.

## The stress-sleep-sweat cycle

Night sweats often create a vicious cycle. Here's how it typically works:

- Stress or anxiety raises cortisol levels
- Elevated nighttime cortisol disrupts normal temperature regulation
- You wake up sweating, which interrupts your sleep
- Poor sleep quality increases stress and cortisol the next day
- The pattern repeats

This cycle is particularly common in people dealing with chronic stress, anxiety disorders, or burnout. Your body stays stuck in a high-alert state even when you should be resting. The sympathetic nervous system—your fight-or-flight response—remains partially activated when it should be quiet.

Sleep deprivation itself raises cortisol. Studies show that even one night of poor sleep can elevate cortisol levels the following evening. When night sweats repeatedly disrupt your sleep, you end up with both a cause and an effect of high cortisol.

## Other causes to consider

Cortisol isn't the only reason for night sweats, and it's important to recognize when something else might be happening.

Common non-cortisol causes include:

- Menopause and perimenopause (hot flashes are different from stress-related sweats)
- Infections or fever
- Certain medications, especially antidepressants
- Low blood sugar during the night
- Sleep apnea
- Hyperthyroidism or other hormone disorders

If you're a woman in your 40s or 50s, declining estrogen is a more likely explanation than cortisol alone. Perimenopausal night sweats typically come with other symptoms like irregular periods and daytime hot flashes.

Sleep apnea causes night sweats through a different mechanism. The repeated breathing interruptions trigger stress responses that cause sweating, but the primary problem is the airway obstruction, not cortisol dysregulation.

## What you can do about cortisol-driven night sweats

If stress and cortisol are behind your night sweats, the solution involves breaking that stress-sleep-sweat cycle. The most effective approaches target both your daytime stress levels and your nighttime sleep environment.

**Improve your sleep routine.** Keep your bedroom cool—between 60-67°F is ideal for most people. A cooler room helps your body maintain its natural temperature drop. Use breathable bedding materials and consider moisture-wicking sleepwear if you're prone to sweating.

**Time your stress exposure.** Avoid intense exercise, heated arguments, or stressful work within 2-3 hours of bedtime. These activities spike cortisol right when you need it to stay low. Save difficult conversations and high-intensity workouts for earlier in the day.

**Practice wind-down habits.** Your body needs a signal that it's time to shift into rest mode. Dim the lights, avoid screens, and try relaxation techniques in the evening. These habits help cortisol follow its natural decline. For more detailed strategies, check out our guide on [how to lower cortisol for better sleep](/cortisol/lower/sleep).

**Watch your evening eating.** Large meals or high-sugar snacks before bed can cause blood sugar swings that trigger cortisol release. If you need an evening snack, choose something with protein and fat rather than carbs alone.

**Consider the timing.** If you wake up sweating at roughly the same time each night—especially between 2-4 AM—this pattern suggests cortisol or blood sugar issues rather than external factors like room temperature.
