Jaw soreness or a report that you grind your teeth during sleep is a reason to consider a dental assessment. It is not evidence that cortisol stayed high overnight.

## What bruxism means

Bruxism is clenching or grinding the teeth. It can happen while awake or asleep. The [National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/bruxism) describes multiple contributing factors, including stress, rather than a single hormone explanation. Possible effects include tooth damage, sensitivity and jaw discomfort.

## What to bring to a dental visit

Write down whether you notice daytime clenching, morning soreness, tooth sensitivity or headaches. If someone has heard grinding while you sleep, tell your dentist. Mention medicines and relevant sleep concerns. The dentist can examine your teeth and jaw and decide whether additional assessment is useful.

A guard may protect teeth; other approaches may address habits or related problems. The right combination depends on the assessment. Stress management can be part of care, but a promise to “fix the cortisol root cause” skips that assessment. These options are described in the [NIDCR treatment guide](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/bruxism#treatment).

## How to use wearable information carefully

You may choose to note sleep or daily stress alongside clenching episodes. Keep those observations separate: a poor sleep estimate does not show that grinding occurred, and an HRV change does not identify its cause.

Cortisol+ does not detect bruxism or measure cortisol. Its [sleep feature](/features/sleep-cortisol-tracking/) can organize available sleep information, but neither the score nor a breathing session replaces dental evaluation for pain or damage.
