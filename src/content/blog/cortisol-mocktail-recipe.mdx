## What is the cortisol mocktail?

The **cortisol mocktail recipe** is all over TikTok and Instagram. Influencers say it will lower your stress hormone, fix your energy crashes and help you feel calm.

The basic recipe usually has four ingredients:

- Orange juice
- Coconut water
- Cream of tartar
- Sea salt

But does mixing these in a glass actually do anything for your cortisol? Let's look at what each ingredient brings, according to real research.

## What is it supposed to do?

The theory is that the drink gives you certain nutrients, mainly **vitamin C, potassium and sodium**, that "support" your adrenal glands. Your adrenals make cortisol, so the claim is that the right "fuel" will help them control it.

The drink became popular with people who think they have "adrenal fatigue". **Adrenal fatigue isn't recognized by hormone specialists (endocrinologists) or medical organizations.**

Real adrenal problems do exist. But they're serious medical conditions that need diagnosis and treatment, not a mocktail.

Still, let's see whether the individual ingredients have any effect on cortisol or stress.

## What does each ingredient do?

### Orange juice (vitamin C)

**The evidence here is limited.** A few small studies have looked at whether vitamin C supplements change cortisol responses to stress, with mixed results.

- Those studies used supplements, usually at much higher amounts than a glass of juice
- One cup of orange juice has about 90 to 125 mg of vitamin C

So even if vitamin C helps some people, you'd need to drink a lot of orange juice to match the amounts in studies. And you'd get a lot of sugar with it.

### Coconut water (potassium)

Coconut water is sold as a natural source of potassium and electrolytes. One cup has about 600 mg of potassium.

**The link to cortisol is thin.** Potassium is essential for overall health, and a severe shortage can stress your body. But there's no strong evidence that extra potassium from coconut water lowers cortisol in people who aren't short of it.

What coconut water does offer is hydration. Dehydration is a physical stress, so staying hydrated matters. But plain water works fine for this, unless you're doing hard exercise.

  Keep a glass of plain water by your bed and drink it when you wake up. You get <strong>the same hydration</strong>, for free.
</ZenTip>

### Cream of tartar (more potassium)

Cream of tartar is potassium bitartrate. It's a baking ingredient that happens to contain potassium. Half a teaspoon has roughly 250 mg.

It seems to be in the recipe just to add more potassium. The same points as coconut water apply: potassium is important, but there's no evidence that taking more than normal lowers cortisol in healthy people.

**One caution:** large amounts of cream of tartar can upset your stomach. The small amounts in mocktail recipes are likely safe for most people. But it's still a baking product, not a health supplement.

### Sea salt (sodium)

Sea salt gives you sodium, which is key for blood pressure and fluid balance. **Its link to cortisol is indirect.**

Some theories say people under long-term stress need more sodium to support their adrenal glands. But most people already get plenty of sodium, often too much. The average person eats well over the recommended limit.

A pinch of sea salt in a drink isn't dangerous for most people. But it's unlikely to change your cortisol in any meaningful way.

**People with high blood pressure or heart conditions should be careful about adding extra sodium.**

### Optional extras: collagen and magnesium glycinate

Many viral versions stir in collagen or magnesium glycinate. Here is what they add.

**Collagen** is a protein source. There's no good evidence that it has any effect on cortisol.

**Magnesium glycinate** is a supplement, not a food. The evidence that magnesium helps with stress, sleep or cortisol is **limited and mixed**. It's not proven to lower cortisol.

Magnesium can interact with some medicines and isn't right for everyone, for example people with kidney problems. Ask a doctor or pharmacist before adding it, and how much.

## So does the cortisol mocktail work?

**The honest verdict: it's not snake oil, but it's not a cortisol treatment either.**

What it reliably gives you is morning hydration, electrolytes and a small blood-sugar lift. All of these can help you feel better in the morning.

Any effect on cortisol is **indirect** at best. Feeling hydrated and fed can help you feel better, but that's not a direct effect on your hormones.

Magnesium glycinate doesn't change that. Its evidence is limited and mixed. Everything else is hydration and electrolytes with a trendy name.

If you want a simpler version, skip the sugar:

> **The honest mocktail:** water + a pinch of sea salt. The same basic hydration, without the orange-juice sugar spike. (Skip the salt if you've been told to limit sodium.)

If you enjoy the full recipe as a ritual, there's no harm in it for most people. Just don't let the drink crowd out the bigger habits below.

## Does the mix work better than the parts?

**Nobody has tested it.** There's no research on this exact mix as a "cortisol mocktail". The studies that exist look at single nutrients in supplement form, not blended into a juice drink.

What this drink really gives you:

- Some vitamin C (but not at the doses used in research)
- Electrolytes that matter if you're dehydrated or short of them
- Sugar from the orange juice
- A trendy name that makes it feel like you're doing something

If you like the taste and it helps you drink more, there's no harm in it. But it's not a targeted cortisol treatment. For more on what actually works, see our post on [foods that may help lower cortisol](/cortisol/lower/foods/).

## What actually affects cortisol?

Real cortisol management means tackling the root causes of long-term stress:

- **Sleep quality and length:** poor sleep can raise cortisol.
- **Ongoing mental stress:** anxiety, work stress or relationship problems.
- **Exercise habits:** both too much and too little can affect cortisol patterns.
- **Blood sugar control:** frequent crashes and spikes affect stress hormones.
- **Medical conditions:** thyroid problems, sleep apnea, depression and others.

A drink won't fix these. Better sleep, stress techniques that work for you and a balanced diet are better places to start than any mocktail recipe.
