You slept eight hours. You're still exhausted. Coffee doesn't help. Rest doesn't help.

**This may be what people call cortisol fatigue:** exhaustion linked with a stress hormone rhythm that's out of sync.

Cortisol fatigue isn't a medical diagnosis, and many conditions cause tiredness. So it's worth talking to a doctor if it doesn't lift.

It happens when your body's stress system gets stuck in the wrong gear. Your cortisol might be too high, too low or just badly timed. Either way, the result is the same: deep tiredness that sleep can't fix.

## What does cortisol fatigue feel like?

**It has a pattern that sets it apart from everyday tiredness:**

- **Tired mornings.** You wake up tired, even after full nights of sleep.
- **Afternoon crashes.** Your energy drops hard in the afternoon.
- **"Wired but tired" evenings.** You're exhausted but can't fall asleep.
- **Low stress tolerance.** Small problems feel overwhelming.
- **Brain fog.** It's hard to focus or remember things.

People often call it feeling "bone-tired" or "running on empty".

Normal tiredness goes away with rest. Cortisol fatigue doesn't. **The key difference: this exhaustion may follow your body's daily rhythm, not just how much sleep you got.**

## Why does my cortisol rhythm matter?

**Healthy cortisol follows a daily pattern.** It peaks about 30 minutes after you wake up (this is called the cortisol awakening response). Then it slowly drops through the day and reaches its lowest point around the middle of the night ([StatPearls, *Physiology, Cortisol*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK538239/)).

This rhythm does more than wake you up. Cortisol timing affects:

- **Energy.** Cortisol helps make fuel available to your body.
- **Blood sugar.** It keeps fuel (glucose) available when you need it.
- **Inflammation.** A healthy rhythm helps keep inflammation in check.
- **Sleep and waking.** The drop at night lets melatonin, your sleep hormone, rise.

When long-term stress throws this pattern off, cortisol shows up at the wrong times:

- High cortisol at night can disrupt sleep.
- Low cortisol in the morning may make waking up harder.
- Flat cortisol all day may mean fewer natural energy peaks.

Some evidence suggests that a disrupted cortisol rhythm is linked with chronic fatigue symptoms. **The problem isn't always that cortisol is too high or too low overall. It's that the timing is wrong.**

## How does stress turn into cortisol fatigue?

**It usually builds up over time.** Early on, long-term stress may leave you wired and anxious, with trouble sleeping. Over a long stretch, some people's daily cortisol rhythm seems to flatten, and their energy becomes hard to predict.

You may hear this called "adrenal fatigue". That isn't a recognised medical diagnosis, according to the Endocrine Society. Heavy, lasting exhaustion often overlaps with what people call [burnout](/faq-burnout/), and a doctor can check for other causes.

## What throws my cortisol off?

**Several things can knock your cortisol rhythm off track:**

- **Long-term mental stress.** Ongoing work pressure, relationship problems or money worries keep your stress system switched on. Your body treats these modern stresses the same way it treats physical danger.
- **Poor sleep.** This makes a loop. Poor sleep disrupts cortisol rhythms, and even one short night raises the next evening's cortisol ([Leproult et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/20.10.865)). Disrupted cortisol then makes sleep worse. Long-term insomnia in particular is linked with the HPA axis (your stress-hormone system) being active around the clock ([Vgontzas et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11502812/)). Shift work is a big problem because it fights your natural rhythm.
- **Inflammation.** Long-term illness, autoimmune conditions or gut problems may get in the way of cortisol signals. Your body might make cortisol, but your cells may not respond to it as well.
- **Blood sugar swings.** Irregular eating or high-sugar diets may add to the load. Cortisol helps control blood sugar, so big swings give it more work to do.

**The tricky part: these usually happen together.** Stress can disrupt sleep, poor sleep can raise inflammation, inflammation can affect blood sugar, and the cycle keeps going.

## What can I do about cortisol fatigue?

**Recovery means fixing the rhythm, not just pushing through.**

- **Keep steady sleep and wake times**, even on weekends. Get bright light in the first hour after waking. This helps keep your body clock steady.
- **Keep blood sugar steady.** Eat protein with breakfast within an hour of waking. Avoid long gaps between meals.
- **Cut stress you don't need**, where you really can. Some stress can't be avoided, but a lot of daily stress is optional. Saying no to extra commitments isn't lazy. It's essential for recovery.
- **Move, but don't overtrain.** Moderate exercise may support a healthy daily rhythm. Hard training without enough recovery can make tiredness worse. If you're exhausted, a walk beats forcing a hard workout.
- **Look at your total load.** Recovery happens when your total stress drops below what your body can recover from. That means working on several things at once, not just one.
