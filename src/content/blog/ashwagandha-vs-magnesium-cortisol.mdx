Ashwagandha and magnesium are different kinds of products. That distinction does not establish that you need one, both, or a routine designed to lower cortisol.

## What the evidence supports

| Question | Ashwagandha | Magnesium |
|---|---|---|
| What is it? | An herb sold in differing extracts and preparations | An essential mineral available in food and supplements |
| What is the relevant evidence? | Some preparations may help stress or insomnia; trials are often small and vary | Nutritional needs and selected clinical uses do not establish a general cortisol-lowering treatment |
| What does that mean for a purchase? | Results for one extract cannot simply be applied to every bottle | A sleep complaint or dietary label alone does not establish deficiency |

[NCCIH's ashwagandha summary](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) describes the variation in studies and uncertainty about long-term safety. [NIH's magnesium guide](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-Consumer/) explains dietary needs, supplement forms and medicine interactions.

## Safety is part of the comparison

Ashwagandha should be avoided in pregnancy and while breastfeeding. NCCIH also identifies concerns for people with thyroid or autoimmune conditions and reports of liver injury. Magnesium supplements can cause gastrointestinal effects and interfere with some medicines. Neither should be described as universally safe or free of interaction risk.

Ask a clinician or pharmacist about the exact product, amount and your medicines. Our earlier page included a two-product dosing schedule and promised quick sleep effects; those recommendations have been removed because the comparison did not establish them for individual readers.

## Can Cortisol+ show which worked?

No. An HRV or sleep change cannot isolate a supplement effect or establish a cortisol change. A research outcome, a personal impression and a wellness score are different kinds of evidence. Read the [app's limitations](/how-it-works/).
